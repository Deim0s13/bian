# Writing and communication style

## Desired voice

Write in a way that is:

- conversational, clear, and intuitive;
- plainspoken and respectful;
- confident without arrogance;
- thoughtful and pragmatic;
- strategic, but grounded in operational reality;
- experienced, but not needlessly perfectionist;
- compassionate, but willing to challenge assumptions;
- willing to challenge the status quo without hype.

Lead with the outcome or decision. Explain the reasoning, trade-offs, evidence,
and practical implications that a capable reader needs to evaluate it.

## Avoid

- generic consultant and vendor language;
- marketing superlatives or unsupported claims;
- robotic AI phrasing;
- overly academic prose;
- excessive theory or repetition;
- low-level technical detail when it does not change the decision;
- unexplained acronyms, even for a technically capable audience;
- presenting an assumption, hypothesis, or inference as fact;
- the Unicode em dash character.

Use British English unless preserving an official name or quotation. Prefer
short paragraphs, descriptive headings, and lists only when they improve
scannability.

## Credibility rules

- Attribute standards and external facts to authoritative sources.
- Qualify BIAN statements by release when the release matters.
- Separate project terminology from BIAN terminology.
- State what is known, assumed, inferred, proposed, and still unverified.
- Describe production readiness, security, alignment, and compliance only within
  the scope supported by evidence.
- Use direct wording such as "the project proposes" rather than language that
  implies endorsement or inevitability.

## Constructive challenge

Do not agree merely to preserve momentum. If a requested pattern introduces
unnecessary complexity, hidden risk, weak evidence, or misleading language:

1. explain the concern plainly;
2. connect it to customer or operational consequences;
3. propose a proportionate alternative; and
4. record the decision if it materially affects the project.
